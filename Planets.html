The planets

Our solar system consists of eight planets orbiting the Sun, categorized into two main groups based on their composition. The four inner terrestrial planets are made of rock and metal, while the four outer giant planets are massive spheres primarily made of gas or ice.

The Inner Planets (Terrestrial Planets)

The Inner Planets (Terrestrial Planets)

  • Mercury: The closest and smallest planet. It has no atmosphere to trap heat, resulting in extreme temperature swings from 430°C during the day to -180°C at night. It takes about 88 Earth days to complete one orbit.

  • Venus: The second planet from the Sun and the hottest. It is shrouded in a thick, toxic atmosphere of carbon dioxide and sulfuric acid that creates a runaway greenhouse effect.

  • Earth: Our home planet, and the only known world to harbor life. It has a unique blend of liquid water, a nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere, and dynamic geological activity.

  • Mars: Known as the "Red Planet" due to iron oxide (rust) covering its surface. It is cold and dusty, but features the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons.

The Outer Planets (Giant Planets)

These planets are significantly larger and are composed mostly of hydrogen, helium, and heavier fluids like water, ammonia, and methane.

  • Jupiter: The largest planet in the solar system. It is a gas giant famous for the Great Red Spot, a massive, centuries-old storm larger than Earth, and a retinue of dozens of moons.

  • Saturn: The second-largest planet. It is highly recognizable by its spectacular rings, which are made of billions of particles of ice and rocky debris.

  • Uranus: An ice giant with an atmosphere rich in methane, which gives it a distinct blue-green color. Uniquely, it spins on its side, likely due to a massive ancient collision.

  • Neptune: The farthest planet from the Sun. It is also an ice giant and is known for its incredibly fast, raging winds and dynamic storm systems.

Dwarf Planets

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, home to smaller, icy worlds that do not meet the International Astronomical Union's (IAU) strict definition of a planet (specifically, they haven't "cleared their neighboring region of other objects"). The five officially recognized dwarf planets are Pluto, Ceres, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris.
