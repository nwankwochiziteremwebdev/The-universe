The Sun

The Sun is a yellow dwarf star at the center of our solar system. It is roughly 4.6 billion years old and sits about 93 million miles (150 million kilometers) away from Earth. Holding 99.8% of the solar system's total mass, its massive gravity anchors all orbiting planets, asteroids, and comets.

Basic Anatomy and Composition

  • Composition: The Sun is not solid; it is a giant ball of super-hot plasma (electrically charged gas). It is made up of about 74% hydrogen and 24% helium, with trace amounts of heavier elements like oxygen and iron.

  • Distance: The Sun is about 150 million kilometers (93 million miles) away from Earth. Light takes exactly 8 minutes and 20 seconds to travel to us.

  • Rotation: Because it is made of plasma, the Sun does not rotate as a solid body. Its equator rotates once every 25 Earth days, while its poles take about 36 days.

Energy Production (The Core)

At the Sun's core, extreme gravitational pressure forces hydrogen atoms to smash together and form helium. This process, called nuclear fusion, generates immense heat—reaching up to 15 million degrees Celsius (27 million degrees Fahrenheit). Every second, the Sun converts about 4 million tons of matter into pure energy.

Layers of the Sun

The Sun has several distinct layers where heat and energy are moved and managed:

  1. Core: The central power plant where nuclear fusion takes place.

  2. Radiative Zone: Energy travels outward from the core in the form of radiation.

  3. Convective Zone: Boiling plasma carries heat up to the surface.

  4. Photosphere: The visible surface of the Sun, which is about 5,500°C (10,000°F).

  5. Corona: The outermost, incredibly hot atmosphere that extends into space.

Solar Activity

The Sun's twisting magnetic fields cause dynamic space weather that can sometimes affect Earth:

  • Sunspots: Dark, cooler patches on the Sun's surface caused by intense magnetic activity.

  • Solar Flares: Sudden, brilliant flashes of high-energy radiation.

  • Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs): Massive explosions of plasma and magnetic fields shot into space, which can disrupt satellites and power grids on Earth.
