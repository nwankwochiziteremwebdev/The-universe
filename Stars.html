The Stars

Stars are massive, glowing spheres of plasma held together by their own gravity. They generate heat and light through nuclear fusion in their cores, typically converting hydrogen into helium. Our Sun is an average star, located roughly 93 million miles from Earth.

The Life Cycle of a Star

A star's life follows a consistent pattern based primarily on its mass:

  • Birth: Stars begin in nebulae, which are massive, cold clouds of dust and hydrogen gas. Gravity pulls the gas together until the core becomes dense and hot enough to ignite nuclear fusion.

  • Main Sequence: Stars spend 90% of their lives here, maintaining a stable balance between inward gravity and outward pressure from fusion. Our Sun has been in this phase for about 4.6 billion years.

  • Death: When a star runs out of hydrogen, its core collapses while its outer layers expand. Average-sized stars become red giants before gently shedding their outer layers to form a white dwarf. Extremely massive stars explode in a violent supernova, leaving behind a super-dense neutron star or a black hole.

Classifying Stars

Astronomers categorize stars primarily by their color, size, and temperature:

  • Color and Temperature: A star's color directly correlates to its surface temperature. Blue and blue-white stars are the hottest (reaching up to 30,000 Kelvin), while red stars are the coolest (below 4,000 Kelvin).

  • Size and Luminosity: Stars vary in size from small red dwarfs to massive supergiants and hypergiants.
